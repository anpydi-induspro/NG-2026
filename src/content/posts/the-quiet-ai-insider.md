Walk into any room today and the conversation about AI sounds the same. Agents. Copilots. The future of work. Big words. Big slides.

Now walk into the IT room of a Fortune 500 company that runs Oracle or SAP or Workday. There is no excitement there. \
There is a tired person at a laptop. They are looking at a feature called Smart Match. \
It is supposed to read supplier invoices and decide which purchase order they belong to. The vendor said it would save 40 percent of the team’s time.\
It saves about 11 percent. And it makes mistakes the team never used to make.\
This is the part of the AI story that nobody is writing about.\
I have spent the last 20 years inside enterprise systems. Finance modules. Supply chain. Procurement. Project accounting. \
The work that runs the actual company. Payroll. Vendor payments. The numbers your CFO presents on Tuesday morning.

For two years now, every big software company has been shipping AI into those modules. \
They call it different things. Oracle has its agents. SAP has Joule. Workday has Illuminate. Microsoft has Copilot in Dynamics.\
The marketing is loud. The rollout, in most companies, is quiet.\
Here is what is actually happening on the ground.\
The AI feature ships. The IT team turns it on in a test environment. Then someone from internal audit asks one question.\
 “If the AI approves an invoice, who is accountable when it is wrong?” Nobody has a good answer. \
 The lawyers get pulled in. The compliance officer wants documentation. The auditors want to know how to test the model. The original vendor demo did not cover any of this.\
Six months later the feature is still off in production. The board still hears about AI in the quarterly call.

The companies that did turn it on are mostly the ones with a single, narrow use case. Invoice matching. Expense report flagging. Forecast variance alerts. Small. Contained. \
A human always reviewing. Those are working fine. Nobody writes a press release about them because they do not make a good story.

The big dream, the one where the AI runs your supply chain and reorders your inventory and talks to your suppliers, that one is not happening at scale.\
 Not in production at most companies. Not yet. The reason is not that the AI is not smart enough. \
 The reason is that most organizations have not figured out who signs off when the AI is wrong.

This is the boring truth that most AI bloggers miss. Enterprise software is not a chatbot in a browser. It is the system that moves real money. \
Every action has an audit trail. Every mistake has a person attached to it. You cannot put an agent into that loop without first answering the question of accountability. \
And accountability is a legal problem, not a technical one.

So what should you do if you run a finance or supply chain team?\
Three things.\
First, do not believe the demos. Always ask to see one real customer who is using the feature in production today, with their compliance team’s approval.\
Second, start with the smallest possible use case. One process. One screen. One human reviewer.\
Third, ask your auditor before you ask your vendor. If the auditor cannot bless the workflow, the feature is not ready. It does not matter how clever it is.\
The AI revolution inside the enterprise is real. It is just not loud. It is not fast. It is happening in places you cannot see from a press release.\
The boring people, the ones writing audit policies and approval matrices, are quietly deciding what AI will be allowed to do in your company.

You should buy them a coffee.
