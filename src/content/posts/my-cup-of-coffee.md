## Seven Minutes That Belong to Me

The pantry is thirty seconds from my desk. I time it.

Morning coffee comes first. The grinder coughs, the beans rattle, and for a second the air shifts. Before brewing, coffee smells like nothing in particular — earth, maybe paper. Once the burrs touch it, the room changes. Citrus, then chocolate, then something darker underneath I can never name.

The bean isn’t a bean. It is a seed inside a fruit — two of them, flat-sided, pressed together like sleeping twins inside a red cherry. Strip the skin, the parchment, the thin silver layer, and what’s left is what we roast, grind, drink. A small, complicated thing made by a tree that has no idea we exist.

I think about Kaldi sometimes. The Ethiopian goatherd, ninth century, who watched his goats grow restless after eating berries off a strange shrub. The story is half-invented. I thank him anyway. I thank the Yemeni Sufi monks who brewed it to stay awake through evening prayer. I thank the unknown person in Istanbul who first decided to roast the seed instead of chewing it. None of them imagined a software professional in a glass office, holding a paper cup, waiting for a Slack message to load.

In the morning, the smell is anticipation. I think of the day’s tasks, the standup at 9:30, the project call that will go ten minutes over because it always does. I think about the weekly deployment scheduled for friday that probably will not happen. The coffee, by then, is already half-cold in my hand. I have not noticed because I have been thinking about service now.

Evening coffee is a different drink, even when it is the same drink. The aroma is heavier — partly because the second pot has sat on the warmer, partly because I am tireder and the world has narrowed. The first sip tastes like a decision. *I am staying.* Two more deliverables. One review comment to address. An email I have been avoiding since Monday. The coffee is a contract I make with myself.

Seven minutes, sometimes eight. That is the round trip. Pantry, grinder, cup, walk back. Three nods to colleagues whose names I sometimes forget by Friday. A glance out the window — sky still bright in May, dark by November. The cup warm against my palm. The thought, briefly, that I should drink more water. The thought, more briefly, that I will not.

By the time I sit down, the heat has shifted from the cup to my hand to the desk where I rest it. The screen is exactly as I left it. The cursor blinks in the same place. Nothing has changed except me, and only slightly. I open the email I had been avoiding. I read the first line. I take one more sip — the temperature now perfect, brief — and I begin.

Somewhere, a tree I will never see is growing seeds I will never thank it for. The least I can do is finish what I started.
