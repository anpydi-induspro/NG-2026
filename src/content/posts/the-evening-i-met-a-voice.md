I was in ninth grade the first time I traveled alone.

It does not sound like much now, but back then it felt huge. A bus, a long road, and me on my way to my grandparents’ house with no one to look after me. I remember holding my bag a little too tightly and watching the world go by the window like it was putting on a show just for me.

![The Evening I Met a Voice](https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/800/1*EDFN64QGlnwLumq98NFgMw.png)

In the evening, the bus stopped at a small tea stall. The kind every bus stops at. Tired people, hot tea in small glasses, the smell of dust and frying snacks in the air. I got down to stretch my legs.

And then I heard it.

A song was playing somewhere near the stall. A voice, deep and slow, singing “Woh kagaz ki kashti, woh barish ka pani.” I did not know the words yet. I did not know the singer. But I stood there, frozen, holding my glass of tea, feeling something I did not have a name for.

The voice had a kind of weight to it. It did not rush. It did not try to impress you. It just sat with you, like an old friend who understood something you could not yet say out loud. I was a boy at a tea stall, but for those few minutes I felt older and quieter inside.

Then the bus honked, and I had to go.

But the voice came with me.

These were the days before the internet. You could not type a line into a phone and find your answer in two seconds. If a song moved you, you had to chase it. You had to ask people, listen carefully, and hope.

At home we had a Murphy tape recorder. My father had bought it to play bhakti songs in the morning. I started using it for something new. I would listen, search, and ask anyone who might know who sang that song about the paper boat and the rain.

It took me a while. But slowly, I found my answer. The singer was Jagjit Singh.

I still remember buying my first cassette of his songs from Rajesh Radios. I held it like it was treasure. In a way, it was. That little cassette was the start of something that has stayed with me my whole life.

Since that evening, I have been his loyal listener. Most of my evenings still belong to him. After a long day, when the house grows quiet, I put on his ghazals and let that deep, calm voice fill the room. It still does for me what it did at that tea stall all those years ago. It slows me down. It makes me feel understood without a single word from me.

Jagjit Singh is not with us anymore. But a voice like his does not really leave. It waits in a song, and it comes back the moment you press play.

I never met him. I never shook his hand or told him what his voice did for a nervous boy on his first trip alone. So let this be my small thank you.

Thank you for that evening at the tea stall. Thank you for all the evenings since.

You were the first voice that ever truly stopped me. And you are still here, every evening, keeping me company.
