The dog spoke to me on a Tuesday. There was nothing special about the day. Tuesdays are just normal days. But it was evening, and I had come home from the office, and I was tired. The kind of tired where the day takes everything from you and gives nothing back.

I was sitting in my backyard with a cup of coffee. The coffee was hot and good. It felt like the one thing that had gone right all day.

Our golden retriever came and sat next to me. Close, but not too close. The way someone sits when they might ask you for a favor.

“You look tired,” the dog said.

I did not jump. I think a part of me had always been waiting for a dog to say something. Now that it had, it felt almost normal. More normal than my cup of coffee in my hand, anyway.

“I am tired,” I said.

“You work too much.”

“I have to.” I held up my cup like it was proof of something. “rent. The house loan, car loans…I do not work to live anymore. I work to pay for all the things I bought so I could feel like I was living.”

The dog nodded slowly. It had the calm face of an animal that has never signed a single paper in its life.

“I do not work,” he said.

“Yes. I noticed.”

“I sleep until the sun reaches the floor. Then I move into the sun. When you come home, you are happy to see me, and I am happy to see you, and nobody asks me about my five year plan.”

“That sounds wonderful,” I said. And I meant it.

“Let me ask you something,” the dog said. “Who has the better life. You or me. Be honest. I am a dog. I cannot stay angry for more than four seconds.”

I laughed. “You do!.”

“Tell me why.”

So I tried..

“You are free,” I said. “You make no choices, so you can never make the wrong one. I make a hundred choices a day. You just lie in the sun. There is a kind of freedom in owing nobody anything.”

The dog tilted its head. “That is not freedom,” it said. “That is a fence you cannot see. I lie in the sun because I am not allowed to do anything else.

I almost smiled.

![The Dog Who Had Opinions](https://cdn-images-1.medium.com/max/800/1*3S4Gv6Mxf3wSIqCdTNq-LQ@2x.jpeg)

“You,” it went on, “could stand up right now and walk to the airport. Or sell everything and start again. Or fall in love with the wrong girl. You need to make of your choices, yes. But you have them.

I had honestly never thought of it that way.

“Still,” I said. You rest. You really rest. I cannot remember the last time I relaxed without feeling like I stole the time from somewhere.

This is true, the dog said. “I am very good at resting. It is my only real skill. But I rest because nothing depends on me. Nothing I do matters, so everything I do is easy. When you rest, it costs you something. That is why your rest is worth more than mine. You earn yours. I just fall over and call it a lifestyle.”

“You make my tiredness sound like a good thing.”

“I make it sound like what it is.”

The sun had moved a little. The dog shuffled over to follow it, the way old dogs do, like the sun owes them money.

“What about love,” I said. “You do not carry any of it. No drama. You can have romance and forget the whole thing by dinner. Nobody to let down. Nobody to hold onto. Does that not sound better than what I have?”

The dog went quiet for a long time. Longer than I expected.

“I had puppies once,” it said. “They took them away when they were small. I looked for them for three days. Then I stopped, because a dog cannot hold onto something it cannot see. You think this makes me free. But I am only light because I am not allowed to keep anything. You keep complaining about everything you owe. But some of what you carry is not a bill. Some of it is people.

I did not say anything to that. I held my coffee a little tighter. It was still warm, which felt kind.

“You envy me because I am light,” the dog said softly. “But light is just a nice word for having nothing to hold.”

The car pulled into our driveway. My wife got out and called the dog’s name, and the whole dog changed. The deep thinker disappeared. Suddenly it was just a dog again, happy and silly and good, tail going like a wiper in heavy rain.

It looked back at me once.

“You asked who has the better life,” it said. “I think that is the wrong question. I have the easier one. You have the heavier one. But you get to choose what to do with the weight. I do not get to choose anything, not even which puddle to drink from. So before you trade places with me, remember that part.”

Then it ran off to my wife, completely forgetting all its deep philosophy for the woman with the car and maybe some snacks.

I sat in the backyard a little longer. I thought about my pending work deliverables, and the upcoming meetings, and the airport I would never walk to, and all the choices that scared me. Then I watched my wife bend down to pat the dog , and I thought, maybe not everything in my life is something I owe.

For the first time in a long while, the fear felt a little like a gift. A heavy, annoying, completely mine kind of gift.

I finished my coffee while it was still warm, stood up, and went inside .
